## The horizon is your eye level

Perspective starts with one line: the **horizon line**. It is not necessarily where the sky meets the land. It is the height of *your eyes*. Stand up and it goes up with you; crouch and it drops.

Everything below the horizon line is below your eye level, so you look down on it. Everything above it, you look up at. Before drawing a room, a street or a box, decide where that line sits on your page and draw it lightly right across.

## Lines meet at a vanishing point

Stand in the middle of a straight road and look along it. The two edges, which are really parallel, seem to come together until they meet at a point on the horizon. That point is a **vanishing point**.

It works for every set of parallel lines going straight away from you: rails, the tops and bottoms of walls, the edges of a table. In **one-point perspective** you face the subject square on, so all those lines head to the same single point, while lines that run across or straight up stay horizontal and vertical.

## Far things shrink

The same rule explains why things get smaller with distance. A row of identical fence posts gets shorter and closer together as it goes back, because the lines through their tops and bottoms are both heading to the vanishing point. Use those lines as guides and every post comes out at the right size for its distance.

This is also why a person far down a street fits between your fingers. Their size on the page depends on their distance, not on how big they are.

## Drawing a cube

Draw the front face as a plain square. From its corners, draw light lines to the vanishing point. Decide how deep the cube is, then draw the back edges where they cut those lines: a horizontal one and a vertical one, parallel to the front.

Where the cube sits decides what you see. Below the horizon you see its top; above, its bottom. Left of the vanishing point you see its right side; right of it, its left side. And if the top of a box is exactly at eye level, its top face flattens into a single line.
