## Tone made of lines

**Hatching** is shading with groups of parallel lines. From a normal viewing distance the eye blends them into a grey, so the space between the lines decides how dark the area looks. It works with any tool, including a pen, which cannot shade any other way.

**Cross-hatching** adds a second layer of lines at a different angle on top of the first, then a third, and so on. Each layer darkens the patch a step.

## Spacing sets the value

Keep your pressure fairly even and let the **spacing** do the work. Wide gaps read light; tight gaps read darker; extra layers read darker still. Pressing harder is tempting, but heavy lines get shiny, dent the paper and are hard to adjust. Spacing and layers are easy to control and easy to add to.

A patch should look like an even tone, not a set of stripes. That means the gaps inside one patch stay the same, and every stroke is about the same length and weight.

## Even, quick strokes

Hatch with quick flicks from the wrist or elbow, not slow drawn lines. Start each stroke on the paper and lift off at the end, so the ends taper rather than ending in a blob. Keep them parallel: if they start to fan out, turn the paper so the motion stays comfortable.

To blend one patch into the next, do not stop at a hard line. Let the strokes of the darker patch get shorter or more spaced as they go, so the edge dissolves. When you cross-hatch, change the angle clearly, by 30 degrees or more, so the layers do not create a moiré shimmer.

## Lines that follow the form

Straight hatching works well for flat planes and backgrounds. On round forms, curve the lines so they wrap around the surface like bands on a barrel. This is called **contour hatching**, and it describes the shape of the form as well as its tone.

Here the lines on a cylinder follow the curve of its base, and get closer together towards the shadow side.
