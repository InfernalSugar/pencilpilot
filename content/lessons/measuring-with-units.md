## Pick a unit

Your eyes are good at spotting that something is wrong, but bad at saying *how* wrong. A unit fixes that. Choose one part of the subject and use it as a ruler for everything else.

A good unit is easy to see, has clear top and bottom edges, and is not tiny. For a person, the head (crown to chin) is the classic choice. For a cat it might be the head too; for a building, one window or one floor.

## Measure at arm’s length

Hold your pencil straight out, elbow locked, and close one eye. Line the tip up with the top of your unit and slide your thumb down to its bottom edge. That pencil length is now your unit.

Two habits keep the measurement honest. Keep your arm fully straight every time, so the distance never changes. And keep the pencil flat to your face, not tipped towards the subject, or it will shrink or stretch what you see. This works on a reference on screen just as well as on a real object.

## Count the units

Now step the pencil along the subject, unit by unit. How many units tall is the whole thing? How wide? Where do the big landmarks fall? For example, a standing adult is usually about seven and a half heads tall, with the hips close to halfway down.

Say the answers out loud or jot them in the margin: “body about 3 heads, tail about 2”. Numbers are much easier to check later than a feeling.

## Put it on the page

Decide how big the unit should be on your page, then lightly tick it off down the side: one tick per unit. If you have already marked the top and bottom of the subject, divide that height by your unit count and you get the unit size for free.

This is also the cure for drawings that come out too big or too small. When everything is built from the same unit, the whole drawing lands at the size you planned. Finally, check one or two distances again. It takes seconds and catches the drift before it spreads.
