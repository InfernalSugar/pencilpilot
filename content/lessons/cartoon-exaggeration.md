## Exaggeration is a decision

Cartoons are not careless drawings. A good cartoon is a drawing where someone looked hard at the subject, decided what mattered most about it, and then made that thing **more**. The difference between a mistake and an exaggeration is intent: a mistake goes in a random direction, an exaggeration goes in the direction the subject was already going.

So start the same way you would for any drawing: look, and get the basic shapes. Then ask what you would say about the subject in one word. Strong? Tall? Round? Nervous?

## Push the defining feature

Once you have the word, find the shape that carries it and push it. For “strong”, the chest gets broader and the head smaller. For “tall”, the legs and neck stretch. For a sleepy old dog, the jowls and ears droop further and the eyes get heavier.

Push one or two things clearly rather than everything a little. If every part is exaggerated by the same amount, the drawing just looks like a bigger or smaller version of the original, and nothing stands out.

## Use contrast

Exaggeration works through contrast. A huge chest only looks huge next to tiny legs. Some useful pairs:

- **Big against small**: a large head on a small body, small eyes in a big face.
- **Straight against curved**: a straight back against a round belly, a straight nose on a round face.
- **Long against short**: long arms, short legs.

Each pair gives the eye something to compare. When everything is medium, there is nothing to compare, and the design feels bland.

## Keep it readable

A pushed drawing still has to make sense. Keep the silhouette clear: if you filled it in solid black, would the idea still read? Keep the parts connected in a believable way, so the tiny legs really could hold up that chest, even if only just.

When in doubt, push further than feels comfortable, then pull back a little. Beginners usually stop at about half the exaggeration they meant. A shape pushed confidently looks deliberate; one pushed a tiny bit just looks wrong.
