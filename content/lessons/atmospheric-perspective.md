## The air gets in the way

There is a lot of air between you and a distant hill: dust, moisture, haze. It scatters light, so the further away something is, the more it is veiled by a layer of pale air. This is called **atmospheric** (or aerial) **perspective**, and it is how you show depth with tone alone.

It changes far things in three ways:

- **Value**: dark things get lighter, drifting towards the value of the sky.
- **Contrast**: the gap between their lights and darks shrinks.
- **Edges and detail**: edges soften and small details disappear.

In colour, distant things also turn bluer and greyer, but for pencil the three above are what matter.

## Plan it in layers

Before drawing a landscape, split it into three or four **layers** by distance: the foreground, one or two middle grounds, and the background. Give each layer its own value range, getting lighter as you go back. A simple start is the drill version: four bands of hills, darkest at the front, palest at the back.

Squint at a landscape photo and you will see this clearly. The far mountains are often barely darker than the sky, while the trees at your feet hold the deepest darks in the picture.

## Soft far, sharp near

Draw the far layers with light pressure, soft edges and no outlines at all. Let a distant ridge be just a change of tone against the sky. Resist the urge to add detail to distant trees or buildings: one simple shape per group is more convincing than a dozen tiny marks.

Keep the crisp edges, strong contrast and texture for the foreground. A few sharp blades of grass or a dark tree trunk up front will push everything behind them further away.

## Check the order

When the drawing is done, step back and compare the layers. Is any far layer as dark as a near one? Does a background edge look sharper than the foreground? Either one will pull the distance forward and flatten the space.

The fix is usually simple: lighten or soften the far layer with a kneaded eraser, or darken the front. The order of values, from near to far, is what does the job.
