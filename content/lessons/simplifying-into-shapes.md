## Three forms do most of the work

Look around the room. A lamp is a cylinder on a box. A cat curled up is a squashed sphere. A car is two boxes stacked. Nearly everything can be built from three basic **forms**: the **sphere**, the **box** and the **cylinder** (plus the odd cone or egg).

On flat paper these start as a circle, a square and a rectangle. Add a little depth (an equator line on the circle, a top face on the square, ellipses on the rectangle) and they become solid things you can turn and light.

## Find the forms in the subject

Before drawing, spend ten seconds just looking and naming. “The body of this teapot is a sphere. The spout is a thin cylinder, tilted. The lid is a flat cylinder with a small ball on top.” Ignore patterns, textures and small bumps: they sit on the forms, they are not forms themselves.

Ask two questions of every part: which basic form is it closest to, and which way is it pointing? A cylinder pointing at you looks very different from one lying sideways, so the direction matters as much as the shape.

## Build loosely, then refine

Draw the forms lightly and quickly, overlapping each other as much as they need to. It does not matter that the sphere’s line runs right through the spout; those construction lines will be erased or covered later.

Only once every form sits in the right place and at the right size do you start joining them into one outline: smooth where a form flows into the next, crisp where one part sits on top of another. Details go on last, and they will look right because they sit on a solid base.

## Make them simple on purpose

The point of this step is to be *simpler* than the subject, not just a rough copy of it. If you catch yourself drawing a wobbly sphere because the real object is slightly dented, stop and draw a clean sphere instead. The dent can come back at the end.

Simple shapes are easy to compare, easy to fix and easy to exaggerate. That last part is useful too: pushing a shape slightly (a rounder sphere, a longer box) is often what makes a drawing feel lively rather than traced.
