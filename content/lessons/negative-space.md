## The shapes in between

The subject is the **positive** shape. Everything around it, and every gap inside it, is **negative space**: the sky between the branches, the triangle between an arm and a waist, the box of air under a chair seat.

Negative shapes are real shapes with edges and corners, exactly like the subject. The only difference is that nobody ever thinks to look at them.

## Why it works

When you draw a chair, your brain already “knows” what chairs look like: four legs, square seat. That knowledge quietly overrides what is in front of you, so legs come out too straight and seats too square.

Your brain has no ready-made idea of what the gap under a chair looks like. So when you draw the gap, you have to actually look at it. That is why negative shapes are usually drawn more accurately than the objects around them.

## Pick the useful gaps

Not every bit of background helps. Look for gaps that are **closed** or nearly closed, so they have a clear shape of their own: between legs, between a handle and a mug, between a tail and a body, between two branches. Small, enclosed gaps are the easiest to judge.

Name each one in simple terms. “A tall thin triangle.” “A box, wider than it is tall.” “A lopsided D.” Then draw that shape, and let its edges become the edges of the subject.

## Check with the gaps

Negative space is also the best checking tool you have. When a drawing feels off but you cannot see why, stop looking at the subject and compare the gaps instead. If the triangle under an arm is fatter in your drawing than in the reference, the arm or the waist is in the wrong place.

Fix the gap and the subject is fixed with it. For a final check, squint and lightly shade the main negative shapes: errors jump out when the gaps stand on their own.
