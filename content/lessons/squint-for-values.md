## Why squinting works

When you look at a reference with wide-open eyes, you see detail, texture and colour, and all of that hides the pattern of light and dark. Squinting lets in less light and blurs the image, so small differences disappear and only the big value shapes remain.

It feels silly at first. It is also one of the most reliable tricks artists use.

## Sort into three

While squinting, sort everything you see into just three groups: **light**, **middle** and **dark**. Do not worry about the in-between tones yet. Ask of each area: which of the three does it belong to?

You will often find that areas you thought were different, like a shadowed face and a dark background, merge into one shape. That is useful information: in your drawing, they should be close in value too.

## Make a thumbnail

Before you shade, draw a tiny version of the reference, about the size of a matchbox, using only the three values. Paper for light, one even grey for middle, your darkest tone for dark. Give it two minutes at most.

The thumbnail is a map. When you shade the real drawing, you already know where every light and dark shape goes, so you are not deciding and drawing at the same time.

## Darks first, light is free

On the real drawing, put the dark shapes in first: they have the most impact and they anchor everything else. Then lay in the middle tone. The light areas are already done; they are the paper you left alone.

Once the three values are in the right places, you can soften edges and add in-between tones. The pattern underneath will still hold the picture together.
