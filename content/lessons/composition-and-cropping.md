## Decide before you draw

**Composition** is where things sit inside the frame: how big the subject is, where it is placed, and what the edges of the page cut off. It is decided in the first ten seconds of a drawing, whether you think about it or not. If you do not choose, you usually end up with a small subject floating in the middle of the page.

The fix is cheap: try a few layouts small, before committing to the big one.

## Thumbnails: small, fast, three values

A **thumbnail** is a tiny drawing, about the size of a matchbox, that only plans the layout. Draw a little frame in the same proportions as your page, then fill it with the subject in just three values: light, middle and dark. No details, no outlines to speak of, just big shapes.

Give each one 30 seconds and make three or four, each different: subject big or small, left or right, high or low, cropped or whole. Then pick the one that reads best when you squint at the row.

## Off centre, on the thirds

A subject in the exact middle of the frame tends to feel static, like a passport photo. Moving it off centre gives the picture direction and room to breathe.

A simple starting point is the **thirds**: divide the frame into three, across and down, and put the subject (or its most important part, such as the eyes in a portrait) on one of those lines or where two of them cross. Put a horizon on the upper or lower third rather than halfway. It is a guide, not a law, but it beats the middle almost every time.

If the subject faces or moves in a direction, leave more space in front of it than behind.

## Crop with confidence

You are allowed to cut things off. Letting the subject run off one or two edges makes it feel big and close, and fills the page with the interesting part instead of empty background. Crop decisively, through the middle of a shape, not right at its edge.

Watch for **tangents**: places where an edge of the subject just touches the frame or another edge, without clearly overlapping or clearly separating. A head that kisses the top of the page or a hill that exactly meets a tree trunk creates an awkward visual knot. Either overlap clearly or leave a clear gap.
