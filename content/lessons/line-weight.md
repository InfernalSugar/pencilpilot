## One weight looks flat

**Line weight** is how thick and dark a line is. When every line in a drawing has the same weight, the eye has no clues about depth: the near edge and the far edge look equally important, and the whole thing reads like a diagram.

Varying the weight is one of the cheapest ways to add depth. You do not need any shading at all. The same outline, drawn with a few heavier and a few lighter lines, suddenly sits in space.

## Where to press harder

Three rules cover most situations:

- **Near is heavier, far is lighter.** Edges close to you get the bolder line; things in the distance get a thin, light one.
- **In front is heavier at overlaps.** Where one shape passes in front of another, press a little harder on the front shape right at the overlap. The eye reads it as “this one is on top”.
- **Shadow side is heavier.** The underside of a form, or the side away from the light, gets a thicker line. The lit side gets a thinner one, sometimes almost nothing.

You do not need to apply all three everywhere. Pick the one that matters most for the part you are drawing.

## Swell and taper

The most useful line is one that changes weight along its length. Start the stroke light, press into the middle where the form turns away from the light, and lift off at the end so it tapers. The pressure change should be smooth, like a car speeding up and slowing down, not a sudden step.

Practise this on its own: long strokes that go thick to thin, and thin to thick to thin. Keep your arm moving at an even speed and change only the pressure.

## Keep it light until the end

Put down your whole drawing in light lines first. Heavy lines are hard to erase and they commit you early. Once the shapes are right, go back over and add weight only where the rules above say it belongs.

Then stop. If more than about a third of your lines are heavy, the contrast is lost again. A few bold strokes do more work than many.
