## Compare with straight lines

On its own, a slanted line is hard to judge. Next to a perfectly vertical or horizontal line, it is much easier: you can see at once whether it leans a little or a lot. So every time you check an angle, compare it with one of those two.

The edges of your page are a handy, always-there vertical and horizontal.

## Copy the tilt with your pencil

Hold your pencil at arm’s length and line it up along the edge you want to measure in the reference. Keep it flat to your face, as if it were lying on a pane of glass between you and the subject. Do not let it tip towards or away from you, or the angle changes.

Now freeze your wrist and bring the pencil down over your drawing, still at the same tilt. Does your line match it? If not, which way is it off?

## Think in clock times

If you find it hard to hold the pencil still, name the angle instead. Picture a clock face: a line pointing to 12 is vertical, to 3 is horizontal, and a line pointing to 2 is a moderate slope up to the right. “This roof edge is about 2 o’clock” is easy to remember while your eyes move to the page.

## Small angles add up

A few degrees off sounds harmless, but over a long line it moves the end point a long way, and every shape attached to it moves too. Check the main angles early, while your lines are light: the tilt of a body, the slope of a roof, the line of the shoulders. Getting those right makes the rest fall into place.
