## Find the action line

Every pose has one main curve running through it: from the head, down the spine, out through the leg that carries the weight. That curve is the **action line**. It is what makes a figure look like it is leaning, reaching or running instead of standing to attention.

Before you draw anything else, look for it. Ask: where does the movement start, and where does it end? Most action lines are a gentle **C** curve or a flowing **S** curve. Very few are straight.

## Draw it in one sweep

Draw the action line with your whole arm, in one go, fast. A slow line comes out wobbly and careful, which is the opposite of movement. If the first sweep misses, draw a second one next to it rather than fixing the first.

Exaggerate a little. If the figure leans, lean it more. Gesture drawings almost always come out stiffer than the pose, so pushing the curve slightly lands you closer to the truth.

## Hang the body on it

Now add the big masses along the line: an oval for the head, a bigger one for the ribcage, a smaller one for the hips. Let them tilt with the curve, not sit level. Then add the limbs as simple lines, with a quick mark at the elbows and knees.

Keep the weight in mind. The leg that carries the body usually sits under the head, more or less, and the action line often runs down into it. If the figure looks like it is about to tip over, check that leg first.

## Stop at 30 seconds

A time limit is a feature, not a punishment. With only 30 seconds you cannot draw fingers or buttons, so you are forced to see the whole pose at once. That is the skill being trained.

When the time is up, stop, even mid-line, and move on to the next pose. Ten quick gestures teach more than one slow one. Keep the ones that feel alive, and notice what they have in common: usually a clear curve and nothing extra.
