## When the corner faces you

In one-point perspective you look at the front of a box square on. Turn the box so a **corner** points at you, and neither face is square to you any more. The left face now recedes to the left and the right face recedes to the right. Each set of edges gets its own vanishing point, both sitting on the horizon line.

This is how most buildings, furniture and boxes look in everyday life, because we rarely stand perfectly square to anything.

## Start with the nearest edge

Draw the horizon line, then put a vanishing point near each end of it. Next draw the box’s **nearest vertical edge**: the corner closest to you. Its height and position set the size of the whole box.

From the top and bottom of that edge, draw light lines to both vanishing points. You now have a sort of open book lying on its spine, waiting for the box to be cut out of it.

## Cut the box out

Decide how wide each side is and draw a vertical line on each side to close it off. Verticals always stay **truly vertical** in two-point perspective; only the horizontal edges slant.

For the top (or bottom) face, draw lines from the tops of those two new edges to the *opposite* vanishing points. Where they cross is the far corner. If the box sits below the horizon you see its top; the closer the box is to the horizon, the thinner that top face becomes.

## Keep the points far apart

If the two vanishing points are close together, boxes look stretched and distorted, like a photo through a fish-eye lens. Put them far apart, often right at the edges of the page or even off it, and boxes look calm and natural.

A good test: the nearest corner of a box, seen from above, should not look sharper than a right angle. If the top face looks like a narrow arrowhead pointing at you, push the vanishing points further out.
