## Squint to a blob

Half-close your eyes at the reference until the details melt away. What is left is a single dark or light shape: the **silhouette**. That shape is what people read first in any drawing, long before they notice an eyelash or a button.

If the silhouette is right, a drawing reads as correct even with rough details. If it is wrong, no amount of detail rescues it.

## Block in with simple shapes

Start with the largest masses, drawn as simple geometry: a circle for a head, an oval or box for a body, a triangle for a tail or roof. Draw them lightly and quickly. You are not committing to anything yet.

Compare each block with the reference. Is the body really twice as big as the head? Does the oval tilt? It is far easier to move a circle than a finished eye.

## Check the empty spaces

Look at the shapes *around* the subject too: the gap between a cat’s leg and its tail, the triangle of sky under an arm. These **negative shapes** are often easier to judge than the subject itself, because your brain has no symbol for them. If a gap in your drawing is a different shape from the gap in the reference, the edges around it need adjusting.

## Refine, big to small

Only once the silhouette matches, start carving: join the blocks into one smooth outline, then add medium shapes (ears, a beak, a handle), and finish with small details. At each step, stop and compare the silhouette again. Details should sit on a correct shape, not replace one.
