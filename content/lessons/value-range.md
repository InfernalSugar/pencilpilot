## What value means

**Value** is how light or dark something is, ignoring its colour. A red apple and a green leaf can be exactly the same value. When people say a drawing “pops” or looks solid, they are usually reacting to a good range of values, not to the lines.

## Set the two ends first

Find the single darkest spot in the reference. It might be a pupil, a deep shadow under a chin or a gap between leaves. Make that spot properly dark in your drawing, as dark as your tool allows. Then find the lightest area and protect it: leave it as clean paper.

Now you have both ends of the scale on the page, and every other tone has something to be compared with. Without them, most drawings drift into a narrow band of medium greys and look flat.

## Fit everything between

Go through the middle tones by comparing, not guessing. Is this shadow lighter or darker than that one? Is the background closer to the white end or the black end? Values are relative: a mid grey looks dark next to white and light next to black.

Using the five-step scale above as a checklist helps. Most areas belong to one of those five boxes.

## Build darks in layers

Darken gradually. Two or three light layers are easier to control than one heavy one, and you can always add more, while taking dark away is much harder. Stop once an area matches the reference.

Keep your very darkest tone for the few places that truly need it. If everything is dark, nothing is, and the drawing loses the contrast that made the darks special.
